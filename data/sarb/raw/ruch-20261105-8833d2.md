### Q1. Oil shock: what surprised the SARB most?

Matheus — Question:

Looking back to March, when the shock began, what surprised you most about how the oil shock passed through to the economy, especially inflation? And what played out as expected, based on previous research?

David Fowkes — Answer:

That's a good question.

I think what was always uncertain was how long this shock would last. It could have lasted three months, six months, nine months, or whatever.

If you had asked me in March whether oil prices would still be close to $100 in November, I probably would have said no. I thought it would be more like 2022, when prices unwound somewhat faster.

Of course, we kept emphasizing uncertainty. It's always boring to hear central bankers complain about uncertainty, but there you go.

We don't really have a settled view yet on whether we're seeing second-round effects.

We've definitely seen some pressure in services, but at least part of that pressure was probably there anyway. It would have been an issue even without the shock.

Headline inflation is doing what it's doing. It's not very nice to see, but so much of it is driven by fuel, in various ways. That seems to be the kind of shock people would have experienced regardless of what we did with monetary policy.

Next year is going to be the really interesting part.

Do we get a strong deceleration as the base effects unwind? Or does some other risk get in the way and prevent inflation from coming back to target in an efficient way?

My biggest surprise has really been how long-lasting this shock has been.

When we started hearing about potential peace deals in June and July, I thought that would make political sense for the Americans. And I guess it would have been politically sensible, but it hasn't happened.

Franz Ruch — Additional comments:

If I could add something, although it's not directly related to your question, one thing I'm trying to understand is how oil price volatility passes through to consumer prices.

We're seeing lots of upward and downward moves, which are relatively straightforward to understand in terms of first-round effects.

But we've entered a very volatile period, and I'm curious whether you have any views on how that volatility transmits into prices.

I'm also interested in how you're interpreting what happened with oil prices around the US midterm elections.

David Fowkes — Additional comment:

One last thing.

I do wish inflation expectations among sophisticated analysts had moved less.

It's understandable that households and other respondents would react to the shock, but I would have liked sophisticated analysts to look at us and say: this is a shock, it's going to last about a year, but longer-term inflation should remain unchanged, at the target.

It hasn't been quite as encouraging as I would have liked. We'll just have to keep working on that.

Matheus — Response:

I'm afraid I don't have an answer to your first question about volatility and price transmission. It's a very good question, but I haven't seen recent work on that.

Regarding oil prices, my impression — and Lucas can complement this — is that everyone was concerned about a potential escalation of the attacks after the US midterms.

With the election results and the headlines coming from the Trump administration immediately afterward, I think that risk premium in oil prices came down very quickly.

Now investors are trying to assess whether that change is sustainable.

With the Trump administration, it's always very difficult to distinguish meaningful signals from noise.

My impression is that the recent pressure that pushed oil prices toward $120 had a lot to do with fears of an escalation around the midterms, which, at least so far, hasn't materialized.

### Q2. Why has inflation reacted similarly to 2022 despite better initial conditions?

Matheus — Question:

If I can follow up, something that caught my attention, with the benefit of hindsight, is how inflation has behaved over the last few months.

When the shock hit, the standard playbook for a supply shock was to ask whether it was temporary or permanent and to assess the starting point of the economy.

You correctly placed a lot of weight on the starting point.

But even with a much better starting point, when you compare the behavior of inflation and inflation expectations with 2022, the magnitudes look quite similar — obviously in terms of changes rather than levels.

And this time the rand is helping, core goods inflation is helping, and food inflation is close to historical lows.

In 2022, monetary policy was around three percentage points below neutral. This time, the stance was already moderately restrictive.

So I'm trying to understand what's driving that similarity.

Is it mainly the size, nature and persistence of the shock? Or could monetary policy be less restrictive than the numbers suggest?

David Fowkes — Answer:

In 2022 and 2023, there were many more things going wrong, as you rightly say.

Food inflation was going wrong. The exchange rate was going wrong. There was obviously the fuel component.

And I think something that tends to be underappreciated is how much South Africa itself was going wrong during that period.

We had a large increase in the country risk premium, very high borrowing costs and lots of domestic problems — the riots, load shedding and all of that.

This time around, the broad South African macro story is much more positive.

The macroeconomic reset has gone well. Country risk has come down substantially.

Food prices are actually favorable rather than unfavorable.

And the exchange rate has been considerably more resilient than we expected.

So you've got this one shock, and when you look at how the CPI has responded so far, it seems relatively contained within that shock.

There's an interesting story about services, but I don't think that story is being driven primarily by the oil shock.

Then the question is how you calibrate monetary policy to make sure inflation returns to target after the shock has passed.

So far, that's meant 50 basis points of tightening.

In 2022–23, we had to move by several hundred basis points. But as you say, we had a completely different starting point.

We're also asking ourselves which policy mistake we would rather make.

You never know exactly what's going to happen, and there's a lot of uncertainty.

In July, I think we were somewhat concerned that we could tighten policy, the shock could unwind quickly, and then we'd find ourselves having unnecessarily punished the economy.

By September, the decision became relatively straightforward.

The shock wasn't unwinding, and other central banks were also starting to tighten. It was clear that we wanted to tighten a bit more.

I don't think the policy position looks particularly bad.

We've added some restrictiveness now. If more bad news comes, we can tighten a little further. If good news comes, that additional restrictiveness should help bring inflation down.

I understand your question, and I agree with much of the underlying observation.

### Q3. Core goods versus core services: are second-round effects emerging?

Matheus — Question:

There's recent research from the SARB showing stylized facts from previous inflationary shocks.

Typically, the impact is much larger on core goods than on the core services component.

This time it's different.

Core goods inflation is very well behaved, potentially because of the rand or China.

Meanwhile, core services inflation is under pressure, with readings that are historically quite elevated.

How do you interpret that divergence?

Is this evidence of second-round effects — through transport costs and other services — or is it mainly something that was already happening in the economy before the oil shock?

David Fowkes — Answer:

I agree with your assessment of core goods.

It's difficult to determine exactly how much is a China shock and how much is an exchange rate story.

You've also got a very competitive retail sector in South Africa.

Shoprite/Checkers has won a lot of market share, and Pick n Pay is fighting back.

So I think it's some combination of those factors that's producing this very low goods inflation. We certainly appreciate it.

Services are a very interesting story.

I lean toward the view that a lot of what's going on in services was already a problem before the shock hit and would have remained a problem even if the shock hadn't happened.

I may have mentioned this to you before, but after the January MPC meeting, if the shock hadn't appeared, I think the market would have expected us to cut rates quite substantially over the course of the year.

We might actually have disappointed some of those expectations.

We could have been saying that we need to make sure services inflation turns around and starts moving toward 3%.

Over the long run, services inflation is much more closely related to the inflation target and inflation expectations than goods inflation, which is more heavily driven by shocks and exchange rate developments.

Some of what's happening in services is what we'd normally call a first-round indirect effect.

Airfares, for instance, go up very mechanically when fuel costs increase.

But housing inflation, in my view, is not really a second-round effect from the fuel shock.

It's more about housing inflation in South Africa normalizing after having been extremely depressed.

You've also got interesting dynamics involving medical aid, education and other components.

I don't think those are really being driven by the fuel shock.

I think they reflect the fact that people aren't yet used to the new inflation target. Expectations haven't shifted sufficiently lower.

The work of bringing expectations down is now going to be delayed because we've got higher inflation, rather than a smooth path toward 3%.

But I'm very reluctant to look at the services basket and say: there you go, these are clearly second-round effects.

I don't think it makes much sense to argue that the second-round effects of the oil shock fully materialized four months after the shock.

We actually engaged with the research teams that put together the notes that have recently been published and fed into the Monetary Policy Review chapter.

Some of the original language attributed services inflation more directly to fuel prices.

We pushed back and asked whether they really wanted to make that claim, given that services inflation had been a problem beforehand.

They agreed that this was a fair point, and some of the language was revised in the published versions.

### Q4. How has the information set changed since the September MPC?

Matheus — Question:

Since you mentioned the good and bad news that can arrive between MPC meetings, I'd like to ask how you're assessing the developments since the last decision.

The level of oil prices today is fairly similar to what you were seeing at the previous MPC meeting, although the volatility during the period has been extreme.

Current inflation is evolving broadly in line with the latest projections, and growth also looks roughly in line.

We've also had the reduction in the fuel levy announced by the Treasury, which should lower fuel prices in the short term.

How are you putting all this information together?

Is the outlook evolving broadly as expected, or is there something that concerns you?

David Fowkes — Answer:

That's a great question.

To be honest, I'll engage with it more thoroughly once I receive the full forecast results. I'll get some of those numbers at the end of next week, and then I'll really work through them.

But I think you're right.

We've had this big uncertainty around the US elections. Now that the results have come through, oil prices have come down, and the rand has reacted.

As a result, spot prices are very close to the assumptions we used at the last meeting.

I think we had Brent crude at around $95 for this quarter, and that's roughly where we are now.

We had USDZAR at around 16.40 for this quarter, and we're now at approximately 16.39.

So that all looks fairly comfortable.

I didn't really expect Treasury to provide fuel price relief through the budget.

I thought they might simply say that there wasn't any money available for it. But they seem to have found some resources.

That probably means the near-term inflation peak will be a little different.

It might also help inflation expectations marginally.

But this was never really something that monetary policy should have responded to.

It's a current-quarter or next-quarter development.

Whether inflation was going to peak at 5.7% or something lower, I would have argued that we should look through it anyway, because there's very little monetary policy can do about inflation over that horizon.

Perhaps this makes things a little easier.

But overall, I don't think we've had any large surprises.

Maybe the most interesting thing is that services inflation, within core, came in slightly lower in the latest CPI release.

But we're talking about fairly small changes, so I wouldn't get too carried away.

### Q5. Inflation regime changes and the threshold for rational inattention

Matheus — Question:

Shifting to another technical issue, inflation is quite high right now, mostly because of the oil shock.

But the SARB has also published research on inflation regimes.

I remember a box in one of the Monetary Policy Reviews discussing a threshold beyond which inflation becomes more persistent because it starts affecting wage setting and price-setting behavior.

My recollection was that the estimated threshold was somewhere around 5% to 5.5%, although the analysis was conducted before the inflation target was changed.

We're now close to that level, which raises concerns about inflation becoming more persistent.

So I have two questions.

First, is that estimated threshold still valid after the target change, or would it now be higher or lower?

Second, how concerned are you about the possibility of South Africa moving into a higher-inflation regime?

David Fowkes — Answer:

I think you mentioned a threshold around 5%. My recollection is that it was actually lower than that.

This work was originally done partly to help motivate the change to a 3% inflation target.

The idea was that if people expect inflation to be around 4.5%, which was our old de facto target, that's already a relatively high-inflation regime.

Inflation is high enough that people pay attention to it.

They internalize it, and indexation becomes more important.

So when a shock hits, inflation becomes more responsive.

We like the rational inattention argument.

There's a level of inflation that's sufficiently low that people can rationally pay less attention to it.

When shocks come along, they don't worry about them as much because they're used to prices being reasonably stable.

I don't think we'd managed to establish that low-inflation regime when the shock hit.

We were on our way there, but people in South Africa certainly weren't at the point where they could say they didn't need to worry much about inflation because it was generally very low.

Their lived experience was inflation closer to 5%.

We were trying to transition to a better equilibrium, and we hadn't yet arrived there.

The shock means that getting there will take longer.

But this isn't something you achieve over one or two years.

You lower the target, you get inflation down, and over time people stop worrying so much about inflation.

That takes years.

Higher inflation now delays that transition, but it doesn't mean that once people see 5% inflation, inflation can never come back down.

It's not an irreversible outcome.

The base effects should help us considerably.

I'm really hoping we get a period of lower inflation prints, and ideally some undershoots, because that would strengthen the story we're trying to tell.

We could then say: we were hit by a shock, we adjusted monetary policy, inflation came down as promised, and this is the inflation environment you should now get used to.

I think some people are going to want to see that evidence before they fully believe it.

Franz Ruch — Additional comments:

Just a technical point.

That type of empirical work would have been estimated across several inflation regimes.

I'm guessing the sample probably started around 2000.

You would have been dealing with something like a 6% de facto target, or slightly higher, during the 2000s.

Over time, the relevant target gradually shifted lower, eventually toward 4.5%.

So the average threshold estimated across the sample might naturally be around 5%.

I think that threshold has probably changed.

But it would take time before that change became visible in the empirical estimates.

### Q6. El Niño: central forecast or upside risk?

Matheus — Question:

Returning to the central bank playbook for supply shocks, I really liked Rashad's piece on this topic from 2022.

One concern for central banks is the possibility of multiple shocks occurring simultaneously.

We've already experienced a very large oil shock, and now the Southern Hemisphere may be dealing with a strong El Niño.

Countries such as Chile and Peru are already experiencing substantial disruptions.

In Chile, we've seen extremely heavy rainfall affecting copper production. Peru is also suffering disruptions involving copper and fisheries.

South Africa is a different story, because the main transmission would be through food prices.

The initial conditions in agriculture matter a lot.

I know the SARB raised its food inflation forecast for next year in the last projection round.

Has anything changed since then?

And how are you assessing the potential El Niño shock, given your interactions with representatives of the agricultural sector?

David Fowkes — Answer:

I don't think much has changed.

Maybe we have a little more clarity, but if we are going to experience an El Niño shock, its effects on inflation won't necessarily appear early in 2027.

They would probably show up in food inflation in late 2027 or early 2028.

Right now, we're sitting on good soil moisture levels.

The summer crop has been better than expected and very large.

The dams are full, and we've actually been getting reasonably good rainfall at this time of year.

So at the moment, it doesn't feel like a shock is materializing.

Now, it's certainly possible that something could happen.

It could become very dry.

The summer crops growing through the first quarter of next year could be in serious trouble if the rains stop.

But none of that is happening at the moment.

We need to be conscious of the fact that, if El Niño is coming, the inflationary impact would peak quite a long way from now.

The way I'm looking at this is that our forecast suggests we'll be able to start cutting rates relatively enthusiastically fairly soon.

But I'd be surprised if we ended up with a whole series of cuts lined up starting in January.

Part of the issue is that you shouldn't simply assume inflation will move smoothly back to 3%.

You shouldn't assume a potential El Niño shock won't happen.

And you shouldn't assume there won't be another unpleasant exchange rate development if advanced economies tighten monetary policy.

El Niño is another risk we have to consider.

You're right that we've raised the food inflation forecast.

But that wasn't really an El Niño judgment.

It was based more on observed increases in global food prices, which are related to diesel, fertilizers and other factors.

There may be some weather-related elements too.

And then there's the size of the base effect from this year's very low food inflation, which has surprised somewhat to the downside.

So we have higher food inflation in the forecast, but it's not primarily an El Niño story.

Matheus — Follow-up:

So is it fair to say El Niño remains part of the MPC's balance-of-risks assessment rather than the central scenario?

David Fowkes — Answer:

Yes. In the forecast, it's definitely treated as a risk and not part of the baseline.

Reading between the lines, my impression is that perhaps one or two MPC members are behaving as though El Niño is going to happen and they need to worry about it.

But the majority of the committee seems to be saying: I'm prepared to consider this as a risk, but it's not guiding my decision right now.

That's my reading of the situation.

Franz Ruch — Additional comments:

And if I can add something, we're only just getting into the planting season now.

It's still very early.

We should know much more about the baseline by January or February than we do today.

At this stage, it's really a risk-assessment issue.

### Q7. Reaction function: forecasts versus inflation expectations

Lucas — Question:

I'd like to ask something about the transition from the old inflation target of 4.5% to 3%.

I think that is a very significant change.

Matheus and I have discussed this a lot.

To bring South African inflation down to an average of 3% in the long run, you would need a much better performance from services inflation.

You would need a very different behavior from the historical averages of several services components.

At the moment, services inflation is running much higher than would be consistent with a 3% target.

Inflation expectations have moved quite significantly, in my view.

And there's still substantial uncertainty over whether the oil shock is temporary or persistent.

David Fowkes — Clarification:

Can I just ask a clarification question?

Which shock do you mean when you say temporary or permanent?

Lucas:

The oil shock.

David Fowkes:

And how would you see it becoming permanent?

Do you mean a scenario in which the Strait of Hormuz remains blocked indefinitely, or where there's a prolonged war?

How exactly do you define the permanent shock?

Lucas:

I would define it as oil prices remaining close to $100 per barrel for a prolonged period, with a geopolitical risk premium becoming more permanently embedded in prices.

My question is about your reaction function.

Understanding that different MPC members may have different views, where would you focus your attention?

Would it be primarily on the SARB's own inflation forecasts and the QPM?

Would you do your research, build confidence in when inflation will converge to 3%, and then follow that trajectory?

Or would you place more weight on inflation expectations?

For example, if inflation expectations fail to converge despite the model projecting disinflation, would that require additional tightening?

What is the most important piece of information about inflation that you're monitoring during this transition?

David Fowkes — Answer:

I've been trying to tell people that perhaps we've said a little too much about inflation expectations.

We've reached a point where, in some discussions, we talk about the BER two-year-ahead average as though it were almost the inflation target itself.

During this transition, there are risks in doing that.

It could be a lagging variable, and it might not accurately capture where inflation expectations are actually going.

So I think it's important, but perhaps the weight we've appeared to attach to it in some of our communications has been overstated.

I really think the issue is whether additional shocks hit us and make the credibility of a relatively quick return to target less plausible.

It's a very painful experience to keep telling people that things will get better next year, only to keep pushing out the date when they're supposed to improve.

You'll have noticed that we talk quite often in our communications about how long the Fed has been above target, and how long we've been above target.

Perhaps we mention it too often.

If inflation remains above target for 12 to 18 months, I think we can probably manage that.

But if it takes substantially longer, the frustration with inflation not coming down could require further action.

I would also say that, as part of the move to the new target, a major part of the strategy is to keep interest rates above neutral and use those positive real rates to bring inflation down.

Once again, we have the advantage of starting with some restrictiveness.

I'm fairly confident inflation will start falling in a few months.

As inflation declines and those real rates remain elevated, you can become more confident that the stance is sufficient to reduce services inflation and some of the deeper underlying inflation pressures.

I'd also add that we're doing some very interesting engagement with price setters.

We're speaking to organizations such as Eskom and to schools, including through representative associations.

These are institutions that still set prices as though inflation were structurally higher.

They try to implement relative price adjustments of 6%, 7%, 8% or 9%.

We're trying to communicate that this isn't a sustainable long-term approach in an economy growing at around 1%.

So there's an element of engagement and persuasion alongside the conventional monetary policy channels.

I wouldn't quite call it moral suasion, but we're hopeful that some of this engagement will have an effect.

### Q8. What is the most important transmission channel of the recent hikes?

Lucas — Question:

I'd like to ask a quick follow-up about the recent monetary policy response and the 50 basis points of tightening.

In the current environment, what do you think is the most important transmission channel from those hikes to inflation?

Is it the conventional channel, where higher interest rates make policy more restrictive and widen the output gap?

Is it more related to keeping the rand stable and appreciated?

Or is it primarily about preserving credibility?

How do you see the relative importance of these channels?

David Fowkes — Answer:

I think there are three big channels, and they're all working for us.

First, policy has helped keep the exchange rate in check.

We have a relatively stable rand, which is on the appreciated side.

Second, we're making sure there isn't enough demand in the economy for businesses to pass through all of these cost increases.

We're keeping demand on a short leash.

If you're a business trying to raise prices substantially to pass on higher costs, you're going to face resistance from customers.

So we're not accommodating the shock.

You could call that an output gap effect.

I'm never entirely confident about how we model the output gap, because it has some rather strange properties.

But the basic idea is that we're not creating the kind of strong demand environment in which firms can pass on whatever costs they want.

I think that's very important.

Third, the expectations channel is working, especially among more sophisticated respondents.

They can see that the SARB is tightening monetary policy and communicating quite aggressively about getting inflation back down.

I haven't heard many people coming to me and saying: you talk about 3%, but there's no way you're serious about it.

What I hear more often is: do you really have to be this serious about it? Because this is painful.

So I think that's probably good for credibility.

I don't really have weights to assign across the exchange rate, output gap and expectations channels.

They're all functioning.

### Q9. Growth versus inflation: how much room does the SARB have to support activity?

Matheus — Question:

Following up on the policy trade-off, growth this year is likely to be around 1%, and perhaps you'll have to revise your growth forecast somewhat lower.

Do you see room for monetary policy to prevent an even larger deceleration in economic activity?

Or do you think weak growth is mostly related to the structural problems South Africa continues to face?

David Fowkes — Answer:

I think that, at the moment, South Africa has a potential growth rate somewhere around 1%.

Maybe a little less, maybe a little more, but it's very low.

So most of the growth problem is structural, and it's not going to respond particularly strongly to monetary policy.

I think the second-quarter contraction took us from a trend growth rate of around 1% or slightly above that to something closer to zero or a little below.

That widened the output gap.

Particularly in July, we wanted to be sensitive to that.

We didn't want to overreact by tightening very aggressively when demand wasn't especially strong and growth had clearly weakened.

There was a good JPMorgan piece about emerging markets today that I thought described the situation quite well.

Many emerging markets are in a position where they don't have particularly strong or overheating domestic demand.

They do have additional inflation, but much of it is coming from external shocks.

So the goal is to preserve credibility, do enough to keep expectations under control, but recognize that you don't need to tighten extremely aggressively to eliminate excess demand, because there isn't much excess demand in the first place.

I think that describes the reaction function of a number of emerging market central banks quite well.

Brazil may be a somewhat different case.

You've got very loose fiscal policy, perhaps stronger underlying demand, and monetary policy that's substantially tighter than ours.

But for South Africa, I think that description is broadly accurate.

### Q10. Is monetary policy still moderately restrictive after the hikes?

Matheus — Question:

In terms of how you characterize the monetary policy stance, would you still describe it as moderately restrictive after the 50 basis points of hikes this year?

Of course, we've also seen an increase in inflation expectations, which affects some measures of real interest rates.

Would you characterize the stance as moderately restrictive, restrictive or neutral?

David Fowkes — Answer:

We've been able to get a reasonable MPC consensus around the expression moderately restrictive.

Every now and then, I think some of the staff would argue that, depending on how you deflate interest rates and which measure of the output gap you use, policy might be closer to neutral.

But I think the MPC's view is that policy is moderately restrictive.

We haven't gone as far as we did in the previous tightening cycle, when the repo rate reached 8.25%.

At that point, you could probably just say policy was restrictive.

But we are above our various estimates of neutral.

And in most ways you could calculate real interest rates, policy is on the tight side of neutral.

So I think moderately restrictive is a description we're still reasonably comfortable with.

If and when inflation starts coming down, we'll have to reassess whether the word moderately is still appropriate.

But for now, I think the language is comfortable.

Franz Ruch — Additional comments:

It's also quite normal for some measures of the real interest rate to look relatively low when you're facing a supply shock.

By design, expected inflation rises temporarily.

If you look at the stance in a broader sense, over the monetary policy horizon, you can still arrive at a moderately restrictive characterization.

Of course, you're always wrong about the precise level of neutral, whatever estimate you're using.

David Fowkes — Question to JGP:

Do you think moderately restrictive is wrong?

Have you seen reasons to think that's an incorrect characterization?

Franz Ruch — Follow-up:

Can I add something to that?

I'm also curious about how you think about the level of neutral embedded in the QPM.

What's your view?

Matheus — Response:

I agree that, looking at the conventional metrics, moderately restrictive is a reasonable description.

My concern is more about how the economy is actually behaving.

Inflation, inflation expectations and core services inflation seem to be behaving in ways that are somewhat similar to a period when monetary policy was much looser.

Of course, these are different episodes and different states of the economy, so perhaps I'm comparing things that aren't directly comparable.

But my question isn't necessarily whether policy is restrictive.

It's whether policy is restrictive enough to bring inflation back to target over the monetary policy horizon.

That's particularly relevant given the change in the inflation target.

Franz Ruch — Clarification:

Is your concern primarily about the change in the target?

Because going from 4.5% to 3% changes the stance required to achieve the target.

Or are you more concerned with the shock itself relative to previous shocks?

Matheus — Response:

It's a combination of the two.

As Lucas mentioned, even under normal conditions, reaching 3% would be difficult without changes to administered prices and the behavior of core services inflation.

You'd also need a relatively stable rand to keep core goods inflation well behaved.

So the change in target increases the challenge independently of the shock.

And the shock adds to that challenge.

At the moment, perhaps the concern is more about the shock than the target change, but I think it's really the combination.

David Fowkes — Answer:

I was recently looking at a chart of real interest rates prepared by an independent analyst.

It went back to around 2008.

What struck me was how high South African real interest rates have been over the last two or three years compared with almost any other point in that history.

I think our stance has been persistently restrictive, and that gives me some confidence that we'll get to 3%.

But look, if it turns out that our forecast is completely wrong, and instead of seeing inflation around 3.1% at the end of next year we're seeing much higher numbers, then we're going to have to do something else to bring inflation down.

The real prize here is to convince everyone, during the governor's tenure, that 3% actually means 3%.

Inflation should average around that number, sometimes a little above and sometimes a little below.

We don't want to be a country where 3% really means 4%, or something like that.

Anchoring expectations at 3% is something we need to achieve during the remainder of his tenure.

Matheus — Follow-up:

The real rate measure you mentioned — was it an average of different real interest rate metrics, or one particular measure?

Do you have a preferred real rate metric?

David Fowkes — Answer:

It was just a chart I happened to see, and I can't remember exactly how it was calculated.

It might have used an ex-post real interest rate, given that it was a historical comparison.

I've never really settled on one exact right way of calculating real interest rates.

But I'm fairly adamant that using current-month inflation introduces too much volatility, which isn't particularly useful for monetary policy.

Whichever way you calculate it, though, I think you'll find that real interest rates over the last four or five years have been higher than they were for a long time before that.

From around 2010 onward, real rates were generally much lower than they are now.

Starting in 2022, we increased real rates substantially, and there hasn't been anything close to a full reversal of that tightening.

So real rates have remained relatively high.

### Q11. Does the governor's statement imply that the hiking cycle is over?

Matheus — Question:

Last week, the governor mentioned that the prevailing policy settings are sufficient.

My interpretation is that, given the information currently available, there may not be a need for additional hikes.

Does that mean the bar for another rate increase is now higher?

And what would it take for the MPC to hike again?

Conversely, what would you need to see before cutting rates?

David Fowkes — Initial reaction:

This is amazing.

You're the second person to bring that up to me.
